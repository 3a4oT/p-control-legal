01 Who we are

p-control is published by Petro Rovenskyi, an individual developer, who is the data controller for everything described on this page. There is no company behind it and no third-party operator.

Privacy contact: privacy@control.rovenskyi.com. Every request under §11 goes to that address and is answered by a person.

02 What p-control is

p-control runs on an Android TV and helps a parent block apps and enforce time limits on that device. A parent manages the rules from a web panel at control.rovenskyi.com, which talks to the TV over an encrypted connection to a server we operate. There is no separate mobile app.

Managing a household needs an account. It is an email address with either a password you choose or a Google account you sign in with, and it exists so that rules belong to a person rather than to whoever is holding a phone. Creating one adds your email address, your password stored only as a hash or your Google account's identifier, and a browser session, to everything §5 lists. It changes nothing about what the device collects or sends.

Telegram is optional. A parent may link a Telegram account to receive approval requests and alerts in a chat, and may unlink it again; nothing requires it, and a household that never links one loses no feature it needs. Where this page mentions Telegram, it describes a channel you chose, not a step you must take.

03 What we never collect

p-control reads which app is in the foreground, using Android’s Usage Access permission, so it can tell whether that app should be blocked and how long it has been open. An app identifier and a duration is the entirety of what this app is ever allowed to record about what happens on the device. The rest of what §4 sends describes the apps themselves and the television they are on — never what anyone does inside an app.

Never collected, in any version: what you type, what is on your screen, screenshots, your clipboard, the addresses of pages you visit, file names, your location, your contacts, your microphone or your camera. p-control shows no ads and contains no advertising SDK, and no version of it asks Android for your advertising identifier — the permission that would allow it is removed from the app before it is built, so the request cannot be made even by accident. This is a binding rule on every p-control client, not a description of the current release.

The parent’s PIN is stored only as a salted hash, never as the PIN itself, and never leaves the device.

“Your contacts” above means your address book, and nothing in p-control ever reads it. The phone number in §5 is a different thing: it is the parent’s own, handed over deliberately by tapping a button inside Telegram while linking a chat, and the app on the television plays no part in it.

“Your location” above means what a device can tell about where it is — satellite position, nearby networks, anything a location permission grants. p-control asks for none of it and no p-control client ever will. The IP address in §5 is a different thing again: it is the address your browser connects to our panel from, it is recorded only for the sessions you are signed in on, it leaves with them (§9), and we never look up where it is. A device asking for a pairing code has an address too, and §4 says what happens to that one: it is counted for an hour and then gone, and it is never looked up either. Nothing in this product turns an address into a town, a region or a map, and nothing sends it to a service that would.

04 What this device sends

An unpaired device sends the household service one kind of request and nothing else. The only other things it sends anybody go to Google, if diagnostics are on: a crash report if it crashes, and a count-only product event — showing a pairing code is itself one of them — carrying no household, no code and no names (§7, and you can switch both off). To be given a code at all, it has to ask the household service for one, and that request carries what the device knows about itself — a random identifier we generate for this installation, a device identifier Android provides to this app, the device model reported by Android, and what sort of device it is. Nothing about what anybody does on it: no apps, no activity, no accounts, no names. It is not sent only once: a code lasts ten minutes, so a television left waiting on the pairing screen asks again for a fresh one when the old one runs out, and asks again if the request did not get through. It is the same four things every time, and we limit how often any one address, and any one device, may ask.

Those requests go to our web address over HTTPS, so they pass through Cloudflare like every other request to that address (§7), and so do the profile pictures a paired television fetches to draw the “who’s watching” screen — pictures the parent uploaded, fetched from that same address. Everything else the device sends to the household service, including everything listed below, travels instead on the device’s own encrypted link, which does not pass through Cloudflare. Crash reports go to Google instead, not to us (§7).

Two consequences of asking over the web worth stating plainly. Cloudflare handles the connecting address of a device that asks for a code and of one fetching a picture, the same way it already handles a parent’s browser. And we count requests for a code against the address they came from, and against the device asking, for one hour, so that nobody can ask for codes without limit. Two counts, kept under two keys: the connecting address, and the device identifier from the list below. An address can point at a household in the hands of somebody who has other records, which is why it has a ground in §6 and an hour’s life rather than being called anonymous — but it is never turned into a place, never joined to your account or your household, and both keys and their counts are gone within the hour.

The television shows the code, the parent confirms it in the web panel, and this is what travels to the household service:

  1. At pairing — the same four already sent in the request for a code, above: a random identifier we generate for this installation; a device identifier Android provides to this app, which is specific to p-control and to this device’s user profile, cannot be used to recognise it in any other app, and is not an advertising identifier; the device model reported by Android; and what sort of device it is (a television, a tablet, a phone). The device identifier exists for three things. First, so that reinstalling p-control is recognised as the same device returning, instead of leaving a dead entry in the parent’s list. Second, so that if a device already registered to one household is paired into another, the household that loses it is told — the alternative is a television that goes quiet with no explanation, which is indistinguishable from one that was switched off. That alert names the device the losing household already knows about, the time, and nothing about the household that claimed it. Third, when we offer a free trial of a paid plan, so that the same television does not receive one more than once a year: if a household created from this television receives a trial, a one-way fingerprint of the identifier is kept for a year (§5, §9). Nothing new is sent for this; it is the same identifier.
  2. Which apps anyone here can open, and the home screen itself, so a parent can choose which ones to limit without typing package names. Only those: an app that cannot be opened is not in the list, and Android’s own visibility rules mean p-control does not see one either. The home screen is in it because a television spends real time showing it, and an app the device counts time against has to be an app the parent can see a name for. For each one: its name, its version, when it was first installed and when it was last updated, where it came from (an app store, a sideloaded file, or the manufacturer), the category Android files it under, and whether it is part of the system. The list is sent when the device connects and again whenever an app is installed, updated or removed.
  3. An app’s own icon, when the service asks for one, so the parent’s panel shows a recognisable row rather than a package name. The service names the apps it wants an icon for and the device answers with those; it is the icon the app ships with, never anything from the screen.
  4. Which app is open, and for how long — an app identifier, the times it started and stopped, and the child profile that was watching where the device knows which one it was — so a parent can see how time is spent and so limits can be enforced across every device in the household rather than per device. It is sent in batches about once a minute, and held on the device until the connection returns if it is offline.
  5. Which app has just come to the front, as a short live signal each time that changes: the app’s identifier and the moment it happened, and nothing else. It is what makes the parent’s panel show what is on screen now rather than what was on screen a minute ago.
  6. When a child asks a parent to unblock something: the identifier of that one app, and which child is asking where the device knows, so the parent knows what they are approving and for whom. Where nobody has said who is watching, no child is named.
  7. Whether this device is reachable: a message when it connects saying it is online, and one the server is given in advance and delivers when the device stops answering. It carries the app’s version, what this build of it is able to do, whether the permissions enforcement needs are still granted, which version of the app’s first-run data screen you agreed to, and a description of the device itself — its model, its Android version, its language and its time zone. It also says whether the app was running: when it last started, how and when its previous run ended — for example because Android updated it or closed it to free memory — how many times it has had to reconnect since, and why its connection last dropped. No app, no child, no network name and no address travels with that; it is how a household learns its screen was unprotected, and it is sent only once the household has agreed to the first-run screen that names it. A parent sees a television as connected or gone rather than inferring it from silence, and the time zone is there so a schedule set for “after eight in the evening” means the household’s evening rather than the television’s.
  8. After the parent changes a rule: a short acknowledgement that the device received it.
Every item in the list above is sent by the current release; whether the app was running, in item 7, is sent from version 0.4.6 of the app on. The app’s own first-run screen says the same thing in shorter words, and a parent is asked again whenever this list grows — which is what happened for items 2 to 5, for the description of the device in item 7, and for whether the app was running. The way a device asks for its code, described in the three paragraphs above the list, is how the current release already does it.

07 Who else sees it

We do not sell data, and we share it with no advertiser or data broker. These parties are involved:

  • The household’s own parent, through the web panel and, if they linked one, a Telegram chat. That is the product’s purpose.
  • Google (Firebase) — crash reports and anonymous, count-only product events such as app_blocked. These carry no names, no codes, no profile information and nothing about which specific app was blocked. This is the one collection you can switch off and keep the product: Settings → Privacy → Send diagnostics on the television turns off crash reporting and product analytics together, at once, without a restart. Nothing else on this page changes when you do — the rules, limits and screen time your household relies on are not telemetry, and they keep working.
  • Telegram — delivers the bot’s messages, under its own terms, and only for a household that linked a Telegram account.
  • Google, again, if the web panel offers its sign-in button. The button is drawn by Google’s own code, which the panel loads from accounts.google.com when — and only when — the operator has switched that way in on. While it is on, opening the sign-in page tells Google that a browser visited it, before anybody has pressed anything; the panel loads nothing from Google when it is off. Pressing the button hands us a signed statement from Google carrying the two things in §5 and no more. This is separate from Firebase above, it happens in a browser rather than on the television, and a household that signs in with a password never meets it.
  • Hetzner — hosts the service and its database in the EU, on our instructions only.
  • Cloudflare — sits in front of the service’s web address and therefore handles connecting IP addresses and request metadata. Two things a device does reach us that way: the requests an unpaired device makes to ask for a code, carrying the four identifiers in §4, and the profile pictures a paired device fetches for the “who’s watching” screen. Everything else a device sends travels on its own encrypted link to the household service, which does not pass through Cloudflare.
  • Us, when we open a support session. To answer a question you raised or diagnose a fault, someone operating the service can open your household’s own data directly — the same rules, devices, profiles and history the panel already shows you, nothing more. That is a separate, time-limited session, up to one hour, rather than an unaudited look: every household it enters, and every change made while it is open, is written to a log that only we can read. We do not tell you about an individual visit — that is a deliberate choice, not an oversight — but the log itself is something you can ask about under §11.

08 Where it goes

The service and its database are in the EU. Google (Firebase, and Sign-In where it is offered), Telegram and Cloudflare may process data outside the EEA under their own transfer safeguards, including the European Commission’s standard contractual clauses where those apply. We add no further destinations of our own.

Unpairing a device deletes the credentials it holds.

You can close your account yourself, from Account → Close this account in the panel at control.rovenskyi.com/account. It takes your email address, your phone number, every session and your Telegram link with it, and any home you are the only member of closes with you — every screen in it disconnected and everything in §5 erased. The panel names those homes before you agree, and asks you to prove the account again — with its password, or, for an account that signs in only with Google, a fresh Google sign-in — because a session left open on a shared computer should not be able to end your account. It carries nothing that names you, lasts no longer than its year, and you can have it removed as §11 describes.

Two cases the panel refuses, and it tells you which one applies. If you are the last person who can administer a home other people are still in, closing your account would leave that home with nobody able to run it — hand that over to somebody there first. And if your account has acted inside other people’s homes as an operator of this service, the record of what it did is not yours to erase; that is the accountability exception the law makes. In both cases, and for anything else, write to us (§15) and a person answers.

10 Children, and your duty to tell them

p-control is installed and configured by a parent. A child creates no account, never interacts with Telegram, and gives us nothing directly. Everything in §5 concerning a child is entered by the parent, or generated by the household’s own device on the parent’s instruction.

Monitoring software should not be a secret from the person being monitored. By using p-control you confirm that you hold parental responsibility for the household, and that you have told anyone old enough to understand it that this device runs control and monitoring software.

Two consequences of this design are stated rather than glossed over: a child’s own requests about their data reach us only through the parent’s account, and there is no separate handling for teenagers. Whether that is sufficient depends on where you live — the rules differ between COPPA in the United States and national implementations of the GDPR in Europe.

11 Your rights

Some of these you can exercise yourself, immediately, without asking us:

  • Remove a child profile, a rule, or the pairing itself — from the web panel, at any time.
  • End a browser session, or every session at once, and unlink a Telegram account you linked.
  • Switch off crash reports and product analytics — Settings → Privacy → Send diagnostics on the television. This is the objection in §6 exercised directly, so you do not have to write to us for it.
  • Uninstall the app — which ends all collection on that device.
Your right to object to the trial fingerprint. You may object at any time to our keeping the trial fingerprint in §5, and we honour it without asking why. Go to control.rovenskyi.com/privacy/trial-record — no account is needed, including after you have closed yours — and prove the thing the fingerprint was made from: sign in there with the Google account, or enter the pairing code your television is showing. The fingerprint is removed at once. The page gives the same answer whether a fingerprint existed or not, so that nobody can use it to find out whether a television or an account ever had a trial. This is also how to ask for access to or erasure of that fingerprint: we cannot tell whose it is any other way, because nothing stored with it names anybody. The consequence of removing it is only that a new household from that television or Google account may receive a trial again.

For the rest, write to privacy@control.rovenskyi.com:

  • Access — a copy of what we hold about you, including whether a support session has opened your household (§7) and when.
  • Correction — fix anything inaccurate.
  • Erasure — delete a household and everything in §5. You can close your own account without asking us: Deleting your account says how, what goes with it, and the three cases where we have to refuse.
  • Restriction — have us stop using data while a dispute is resolved.
  • Objection — to anything we base on legitimate interests (§6). For the trial fingerprint you do not need to write: see the box above.
  • Portability — your data in a machine-readable form.

We reply within one month. If a request is unusually complex we may take a further two months and will tell you why within the first. Exercising any of these costs nothing and changes nothing about how the app works for you.

If you are in the UK or EU/EEA and think we have got this wrong, you may complain to your national data-protection authority.

12 How we protect it

  • The device’s credentials are encrypted at rest with a key held in Android’s hardware-backed Keystore.
  • The parent’s PIN is stored as a salted hash, never as the PIN.
  • Your password is stored as a slow, salted hash (scrypt) chosen so that guessing is expensive even if the database is stolen. We never see it and cannot recover it — only reset it.
  • Sessions live in the service’s database, not in a cache that can be evicted, and the cookie that carries one is restricted to this site and unreadable to JavaScript.
  • The block screen and the PIN screens are excluded from screenshots and from the recent-apps preview.
  • The device talks to the service only over encrypted (TLS) connections. Two of them are HTTPS to our web address — the requests that ask for a pairing code, which repeat while the screen waits, and the profile pictures a paired television fetches — and everything else it sends travels on its own encrypted link to the household service. Each device is issued its own credential that reaches only its own data; the one it holds while it waits to be paired can reach only its own pairing — never anything belonging to another household — and it is removed when that pairing is confirmed, or swept away with it if nobody ever confirms it.
  • The service and database run in the EU with access restricted to the developer.
  • Opening a household’s own data directly — for support — is not the same access as running the service: it mints its own time-limited credential, separate from anything a household holds, and every household it touches and every change it makes is recorded rather than passing unseen. See §7.

No system is perfect, and we will not pretend otherwise. If we ever discover a breach affecting your data, we will tell affected households and the relevant authority as the law requires.

14 Changes to this policy

If what we collect, how we use it, or who receives it changes, we update this page and its effective date before that change reaches anyone, and we say what changed. Where the law requires it, we will also notify you through the bot or the app.

Version 1.9, 15 September 2026 — what changed. The privacy contact is now privacy@control.rovenskyi.com. Nothing about what is collected changed.

Version 1.8, 15 September 2026 — what changed. Nothing new leaves a television, and before any of it is kept this page says so.

  • When we offer a free trial of a paid plan, a household created from a television receives it only if neither that television nor a Google account its founder signs in with had one in the last year. To know that, we keep a keyed one-way fingerprint of the television's device identifier and of the Google account's identifier — only for those that received a trial, for 365 days, and also after an account is closed. Its ground is legitimate interests, and you can object to it and have it removed yourself at control.rovenskyi.com/privacy/trial-record. See §4, §5, §6, §9 and §11.
  • The history window in §9 now says what each plan keeps: 7 days on the free plan, 90 on a paid plan that sets a window. It said 90 for every household, which was not true of the free plan.
  • Closing an account that signs in only with Google is confirmed with a fresh Google sign-in rather than a password it does not have (§9).
  • §2 says an account can be a Google sign-in instead of a password, and §5 no longer says per-app usage, icons and the device description are received but not kept — §5's own table already listed them as kept, and they are.

Version 1.7, 15 September 2026 — what changed. Nothing new leaves a television. The service now keeps what 1.6 said it only received: whether the app on a screen is running is kept on that screen's record until its next report, and a stretch in which the app was not running is kept with what the screen reported about itself, for your household's history window (§5, §9). It is what the screen's page and the new alert about a screen without protection are made from. The service also keeps whether each stretch of a screen being online ended with the screen asleep, for the same window.

Version 1.6, 14 September 2026 — what changed. A television now also tells your household's service whether the app on it was running: when it last started, how its previous run ended, how often it reconnected and why its connection last dropped (§4). Nothing about what anyone watched is added. The app asks again before sending it.

Version 1.5, 9 September 2026 — what changed. No new kind of information about your household is collected. What changed is how a device first reaches us, therefore who handles it on the way, and one new thing we do with the address it arrives from. The new way of asking for a code arrives with the next release of the app and is on this page first, as this section promises.

  • A device now asks for its pairing code over the web, at the same address as the panel, instead of over the connection it used to share with every other copy of the app. The four things it says about itself are the same four as before; what is new is that Cloudflare, which already sat in front of that address, now handles them and the device’s connecting address on the way to us. And we say for the first time that we count those requests against that address for an hour, so that nobody can ask for codes without limit. That limit is added with this change, not an old one newly worded: over the connection a device used before, there was no address of its own for us to count. See §4, §6 and §7.
  • §4 said an unpaired device sent nothing at all. That was never right, and it says now what such a device sends, and that a television waiting on the pairing screen asks more than once. The profile pictures a paired television fetches come over the web from that same address too, which §7 now says.
  • Each device waiting to be paired is given a credential of its own, good for one thing and dead when that pairing ends; there is no longer a shared one inside the app. See §9 and §12.

Version 1.4, 4 September 2026 — what changed. A new way for us to see a household’s own data, disclosed before it was used on anyone.

  • We can now open a household’s own data directly, to answer a support question — a separate, time-limited session rather than an unaudited look, and every one of them is logged. See §6, §7, §9 and §11.

Version 1.3, 30 August 2026 — what changed. Both changes reduce what is collected, and neither reaches anyone before the release that carries them.

  • Crash reporting and product analytics can now be switched off on the television, in Settings → Privacy → Send diagnostics. Before this there was no way to stop them short of uninstalling. See §7 and §11.
  • The app no longer asks Android for the advertising identifier. The permission arrived through the Firebase Analytics library rather than from anything p-control does with it, and is now removed from the app before it is built. Nothing in the app ever read that identifier; this closes the possibility rather than a practice. See §4.
